# Fatal Frame 2 Crimson Butterfly (2026)

**Score:** 7.5/10

-------


# Review

*Fatal Frame II: Crimson Butterfly Remake* is a game I only learned about recently, following the release of its demo back in March. The demo was interesting, left a good first impression, and definitely put the game on my radar of titles to play. Now that I've had some time with it, I have a few things to say, and I figured I'd write down my thoughts and give it a little review.




*Fatal Frame II* is a survival-horror game in which you play as a young girl named Mio. Alongside your sister, Mayu, you find yourself trapped in the cursed village of Minakami, searching for a way out. Throughout the game, you encounter many ghosts that haunt the village, serving as enemies that you must defeat using the Camera Obscura. The Camera Obscura is central to the premise of *Fatal Frame*, and the majority of the gameplay revolves around it. It's a spirit camera that allows you to exorcise ghosts, as well as capture images of past spiritual events to provide clues for puzzles and navigation.



To start with the positives, I absolutely love the vibes this game gives off. It feels as though you've come across some cursed Japanese VHS tape and are watching something you know is dangerous but is too alluring to turn away from. *Fatal Frame* encourages exploration, but behind every door you open, there's the uncertainty of whether a ghost is standing behind it, waiting to attack. The same applies to the loot. The game practically begs you to pick up an item as it shines brightly on the floor, yet when you go to collect it, the camera slowly zooms in and seemingly asks, *"Are you sure?"* It's a very unique take on exploration and looting in games. In most games, even other horror games in the same genre, you're often encouraged to keep exploring to see if you can find more ammo, that extra piece of health, or that one thing you can sell in the shop. It's nice to see *Fatal Frame II* take that concept and make you question whether looking for that extra item is worth the risk of whatever might be waiting behind the next door. It's awesome, and that's perhaps the best thing I can say about *Fatal Frame II* it's unique, and it embraces what it is



The Camera Obscura adds to this, and man, let me tell you, the Camera Obscura is perhaps one of the greatest weapons in horror games, and maybe even one of the best weapons in gaming overall. The Camera Obscura is the number one reason I purchased this game, and I don't regret that decision one bit. The way this weapon works in combat is that you're able to raise the camera and aim it toward enemies. Alignment points appear, and having a ghost within those points allows you to deal more damage, depending on how many points are present on the enemy when the photo is taken. This is taken a step further with *Shutter Chances* and *Fatal Frames*. When the camera's focal point flashes red, it indicates a Shutter Chance. This means that, when the perfect opportunity to strike presents itself, you can take a picture at just the right moment usually when the ghost is at the peak of its attack and activate *Fatal Time*. This stuns the ghost and allows you to take multiple photos in quick succession, dealing a lot of damage in a short amount of time.
This battle system is very addicting and heaps of fun, as combat rewards you for paying attention to ghost behavior, including how each ghost moves and attacks. It also rewards patience, as the most effective way to deal damage is to take photos just moments before a ghost would hit you, triggering those Fatal Times. The battle system gets even more in-depth with the use of the camera's filters, special shots, and abilities. However, I'll spare the details on those for now and come back to them later.



Outside of combat, the rest of the gameplay loop consists of exploration and puzzle solving. As I've mentioned, the game takes place in a village that you'll explore, along with its various buildings. In many of these locations, finding your way around and solving puzzles make up the other half of the gameplay. The game isn't very direct about where exactly you're supposed to go. Instead, it has you find your way around buildings and explore them in search of clues. These clues occasionally appear as the game's way of confirming to the player that they're heading in the right direction. Combine this with the occasional puzzle that needs to be solved to progress, and you have a game that's roughly half combat and half exploration, with some light puzzle solving mixed in.



Unfortunately, I find that exploration is where the game suffers the most in terms of gameplay.

Before I lay out my reasons for why I believe it's flawed in this aspect, I want to make it clear that I enjoy exploration in many games. The same goes for other games in this genre, such as *Silent Hill*, *Resident Evil*, and *Amnesia*. This game's exploration, however, just isn't it for me.

I'm unsure whether this is something that suffered in the transition from the original PS2 game. I'm aware that the original uses a fixed camera perspective, similar to the original *Resident Evil* games, but I can't comment on how well the exploration works in that version. In this remake, however, exploring isn't very fun, and I think I know why .. navigating the buildings becomes confusing because nearly every room looks similar to the last. This is especially egregious with the Tachibana House, which appears in the latter half of the game. The Tachibana House, as well as Chapter 7 as a whole, really highlights the game's flaws and brings almost everything wrong with it together in a single chapter. The house is a maze, with one room looking like the next. The minimap isn't very helpful, and there's an enemy in this area that can't be fought. You must run away and hide from it, because if it grabs you, it's an instant game over. This brings me to my next point.

This game features large, boss-like ghosts, with one notable example being Kusabi, whom you'll encounter frequently. Enemies such as Kusabi are very unfun to engage with and are more of a headache than something scary.
Similar to the enemy found in Chapter 7, Kusabi cannot be harmed and instantly kills the player if it catches up to them.

I want to note that I played on the *Battle* difficulty, which is the hardest difficulty the game allows you to choose on a fresh save. I didn't die to a single enemy other than Kusabi. I don't say this as a *flex*, because even on Battle difficulty, *Fatal Frame II* is very generous with its items. I had an abundance of healing items, and I wasn't in any real danger so long as I stocked up before setting out on any prolonged exploration.

This further supports my argument that Kusabi isn't fun to deal with, because it undermines one of the game's biggest strengths, its combat. It's also frustrating to get an immediate game over when Kusabi appears. Many of its appearances come as a surprise, and because of the poor layouts and awkward navigation of some locations, you'll likely meet your end simply because you couldn't find a hiding spot quickly enough.
I feel that Kusabi, along with the enemy in Chapter 7, attempts to fill a similar role to imposing enemies in other horror games, such as the notable Mr. X from *Resident Evil 2* and Nemesis from *Resident Evil 3*. The difference is clear, however. Mr. X and Nemesis both succeed in their roles because they act as powerful forces that push the player in the opposite direction without feeling unfair. This comes down to two key factors: both can be staggered, giving the player some momentary respite, and neither immediately causes a game over if you're unfortunate enough to get hit.

Kusabi fails on both fronts, leaving you with a frustrating enemy design that makes me seriously wonder whether the Big the Cat levels in Sonic Adventure are worse than this. They probably aren't, but you get the point. I cannot emphasize how important these distinctions are, as they're the difference between peak enemy design and a design that makes you want to bang your head against a wall.

With my two major complaints out of the way, that being Kusabi and exploration; I'll talk about a few things I'm more indifferent toward but don't mind too much. I feel that the Camera Obscura's filters and special shots aren't as interesting as they could have been. This is more of a nitpick, but I found myself not using the camera's filters all that much, mainly because they never felt necessary.

To explain, the Camera Obscura has four filters that you can quickly swap between: Standard, Paraceptual, Exposure, and Radiant. Each filter offers different abilities and serves a different purpose in combat.

Standard is your normal mode, which can push enemies back. Paraceptual is used to locate clues and offers a long range filter that can blind enemies. Exposure reveals hidden objects and deals high damage, but it has a slow charge time. Radiant breaks seals on doors and objects and has a charge up ability that deals a burst of damage. I stuck with the Standard filter for the majority of my playthrough, and it sufficed without giving me any trouble in combat. As I've said, combat is very forgiving in this game. You have an abundance of healing items, as well as the ability to shake off ghosts if they grab you. Because of this, switching filters and using their abilities doesn't feel particularly necessary. Most enemies aren't that difficult to deal with once you get a feel for Shutter Chances and Fatal Time. I'm unsure what the game could have done to make the filters something you'd want to resort to more often, but they never felt necessary. Either way, the combat is fun enough to stand on its own.

My other nitpick is that the in game shop is rather lackluster. Unless I missed something, I believe the shop only offers two purchasable consumables, and one of them is something I hardly ever bought. You can buy Herbal Medicine, which serves as your standard healing item, and Reversion Beads, which reset your upgrades and allow you to reallocate them. Aside from consumables, there's a heap of charms you can purchase and upgrade. However, you're only able to equip one charm at a time, so it's unlikely you'll buy many of them beyond whichever one appeals to you the most and its upgrades.

Because of how the shop works, I found that I'd purchased everything I needed from it very early on in the game. As a result, I rarely felt the need to visit it beyond occasionally buying Herbal Medicine. This feels a little awkward because there's not much incentive to fight optional ghosts anymore. The currency they award doesn't mean much once you've got what you need. Of course, I still fought every encounter because the combat is just that fun, but the game often lets you run away from ghosts without any real incentive to fight them unless they're blocking your progress.

## Final Thoughts

*Fatal Frame II* is a very unique game with a cool combat system and awesome vibes. It suffers from confusing exploration and frustrating boss-like enemies, with some combat systems and shop mechanics that could have been done a bit better.

Is it a game I'd recommend? Yeah, especially if it looks interesting to you based on its storefront images and description. I look forward to playing the other *Fatal Frame* games, and perhaps I'll review those as well or rank them based on how much I enjoyed them.
